Building a Worship Song Library and Master Song List (Free Template)

Your repertoire should not live in your head. Here is the master song list to build, column by column, with the key, tempo, theme, and CCLI number for every song, plus a rotation tracker that keeps your strongest songs fresh and none of them lost.

Worship Connect Group ·October 15, 2026 ·6 min read

It is Thursday night and you are trying to remember the key you led a song in last spring. You know the team nailed it. You cannot remember whether it sat in A or B, whether you kept the bridge, or the last Sunday your people actually sang it out loud. You guess. The band relearns the arrangement off the record, and by the weekend the chorus climbs a step too high for the room again. Every worship leader carries a catalog like this. It just lives in your head, a few old text threads, and last year's bulletins.

A worship master song list fixes that. It is one place that holds every song you lead, with the few details that let a set come together in minutes instead of a late-night scramble. This walks through why the list earns its keep, the exact columns to build, how to fill them fast with tools you likely already pay for, and a simple rotation tracker so no song gets run into the ground or quietly lost.

Why a master list beats a playlist and a good memory

Your memory is the most expensive place to store your repertoire. It drops keys, it buries the songs you have not touched in a year, and it reaches for the same handful under pressure. A streaming playlist does a little better and still falls short. It proves a song exists and tells you nothing about how your room sings it.

A real worship song library is a working document, not an archive. When the list holds the congregational key, the tempo, the theme, and the last Sunday you led each song, planning stops being an act of recall and becomes an act of choosing. You scan. You filter. You build. That same list guards your people against the two quiet failures every leader knows. One is the song worn so thin it has lost its weight. The other is the strong song that vanished simply because no one wrote it down.

The columns your song library actually needs

You can build the whole thing in Google Sheets or Excel in about five minutes. One row per song, one column for each piece of information you reach for when you plan. Here is the structure we run in the ministry I help lead, and the version you can copy today.

ColumnWhat goes in it
Song TitleThe song as your team names it, so search finds it fast
Author / ArtistThe writers or the version you learned, for charts and credit
CCLI #The song's unique number from CCLI, your cleanest way to look it up and report it
Default KeyThe key the song is published and recorded in
Congregational KeyThe key your room actually sings it in, often a step or two lower
BPM / TempoThe tempo you play it at, so a set does not lurch from fast to slow
Theme / TopicA few tags like grace, lament, communion, surrender, so you can build to a message
SeasonsAdvent, Easter, baptism, and the like, for the songs that belong to a moment
Last PlayedThe date of the most recent Sunday you led it
Times PlayedA running count that ticks up by one each time
StatusActive, learning, resting, or retired

The two keys are the columns most leaders skip and then miss the most. Default Key is where the song lives on the record. Congregational Key is where your people can reach the high note without straining, and it is the number your band needs. Write both down once and you stop rediscovering it every spring.

Fill it fast with the tools you already have

The detail work sounds heavy. Most of it already exists. If your church holds a CCLI license, your SongSelect subscription is the shortcut for the bulk of these columns. SongSelect gives you the official CCLI song number, the default key, and the lyrics and charts for songs in its catalog, and it lets you transpose a chart into the key your room sings. Pull the number, the writers, and the default key straight from there. Then add the two things only you know, which are the key your congregation owns and the themes the song serves in your context.

One clear word on the license, since it is easy to blur. The CCLI Church Copyright License covers reproducing and displaying song lyrics, making copies, and reporting the songs you use so the writers are paid for them. SongSelect is the separate subscription that hands you the numbers, keys, and charts. Your master list is your own internal planning document, so cataloging your songs in it sits well inside normal use. Keep your usage reporting accurate and current, and you are on solid ground.

The rotation tracker that keeps songs alive

Two columns turn a static list into a living rotation tracker. Last Played and Times Played together answer the one question a set list alone cannot, which is whether a song is tired, forgotten, or right on time.

Sort by Last Played and the top of your list is everything you have leaned on lately. When a song shows up there three weeks running, give it a rest before the room stops hearing the words at all. Sort the other direction and the bottom becomes your buried treasure, strong songs untouched for six months that will land fresh the next time out. Times Played catches the slow drift a single date misses. A song you have run forty times this year is doing real work in your room and has probably earned a season off. A new song with two plays needs a few more weeks in a row before the room truly owns it.

You do not need software for any of this. Update two cells every time you finalize a set. Move the date, bump the count by one. Thirty seconds a week keeps the whole list honest.

Put the list to work every week

A master list is only worth building if you keep it open while you plan instead of buried in a drive. Lived with, it makes every other part of song selection lighter.

When you sit down to pick songs, half the filtering is done for you, and the four-question framework in our guide to choosing worship songs runs faster against a catalog you can see at a glance. When you shape the order of a service, the keys and tempos in your columns are exactly what you need to sequence a set that flows, which is the craft the set lists and song selection track in our Academy breaks down step by step. Once the plan is locked, those same keys and charts drop straight into what you hand your team, so your rehearsal starts from a shared page instead of from scratch.

The church has kept an ordered songbook from the start

None of this is a modern trick. The longest book in your Bible is a songbook, and it was catalogued. Many psalms open with a superscription that reads like planning notes, naming who the song was for, the instrument, and sometimes the tune. Psalm 4 begins "For the director of music. With stringed instruments" (Psalm 4, superscription). The people of God kept their songs ordered, keyed, and assigned long before a spreadsheet existed, because a worshiping church has always needed to know what it sings and how. Your master list is that same instinct, carried into a planning app.

Common questions

What should a worship song library include?
At a minimum, one row per song with the title, the writers, the CCLI number, the default key, the key your congregation sings it in, the tempo, a theme tag or two, and the Sunday you last led it. Add a running count of how many times you have used it and a status of active, learning, resting, or retired. Those few columns are enough to plan a set in minutes and to see the health of your whole repertoire at a glance.
What is a CCLI number and where do I find it?
A CCLI number is the unique identifier CCLI assigns to a song, which makes it the cleanest way to look a song up and to report your usage. You will find it on SongSelect if your church holds a CCLI license, listed with the official key, lyrics, and charts. Drop that number into your master list once and you never have to hunt for the right version of a song again.
How often should we rotate worship songs?
Rest a song for a few weeks once you have led it three or four Sundays close together, so the words do not go stale from repetition. Bring a buried favorite back when it has sat untouched for several months. For new songs, run the opposite play and repeat it a few weeks in a row until the room learns it. A Last Played and Times Played column makes all of this visible instead of a guess.
