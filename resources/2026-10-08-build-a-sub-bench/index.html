Building a Worship Sub Bench: Never Scramble on a Saturday Again

Why a standing bench of subs beats the weekly scramble, where to find the names, how deep to go on each seat, the one-page sheet every sub needs, and what to do when your bench still runs dry.

Worship Connect Group ·October 8, 2026 ·6 min read

You have a sub. One. The same name you have texted for two years, the player who bails you out when someone cancels on a Thursday. Most weeks that is enough. Then comes the week he has the flu, his car is in the shop, and he is out of town for a wedding all at once, and you find out your whole plan was resting on one person staying healthy and free. A bench of one is not a bench. It is one bad week away from an empty seat.

The fix is a real bench, and you build it on an ordinary week when nothing is on fire. Here is why a standing bench beats the weekly scramble, where the names come from, how deep to go on each seat, the one-page sheet that turns a stranger into a teammate, and what to do on the Sunday your bench still comes up empty.

Why a standing bench beats the weekly scramble

The scramble is reactive. A player drops on Saturday, you work the phone in a rising panic, and you take whatever warm body you can get before the service you cannot move. The music suffers, your week gets eaten, and you walk into Sunday already tired.

A bench flips the whole thing. The names are gathered before you need them, the players already know your songs, and each one has cleared your vetting long before a crisis. You make one call instead of ten, and the person who answers walks in ready. When a gap opens anyway, our guide to covering a last-minute fill-in walks the calls in order. A bench is simply how you need that guide less often.

Where the names on your bench come from

A bench is built from people you already have access to, gathered on purpose over a season. Four places turn up most of them.

  • Your own pews. Someone plays at home, drummed in a band in college, or led worship in a past season and never got asked again. This is the cheapest and often the best source you have.
  • Past fill-ins. Anyone who has covered for you before already knows your flow. Keep their number and keep the relationship warm.
  • Players one seat over. A guitarist who keeps good time can carry a shaker-and-cajon groove for a week. A keys player can hold a pad under an acoustic set. Depth sometimes means a regular who can slide sideways.
  • Nearby worship leaders. A text to leaders you trust can hand you a name that would have taken you a month to find alone. Good players get passed between the people who have worked with them.

Run every one of these candidates through a real process before you add them, the same way you would vet a weekly player. Our complete guide to hiring worship musicians covers how to check skill, faith, and background without skipping a step.

How deep to go, and how to keep the bench current

Go deepest on the seats that hurt most when they empty. Drums, bass, keys, and your lead vocal are the ones a congregation feels missing within a line or two. Aim for two or three names on each of those, and one or two on the roles your own team can cover in a pinch. Depth matters more than a long list. Three subs who know your songs will carry you further than ten names you have never heard play.

A bench only works if it stays current. A sub who played your set once in March is nearly a stranger by October. Rotate your bench players onto ordinary Sundays a few times a year, so the songs stay in their hands and your team stays glad to see them. A quick check-in each season keeps the numbers live and tells you who has moved, had a baby, or quietly stepped back.

Done well, this depth also guards your regulars. When no single volunteer carries every weekend, the people on your team get to rest, and a rested team is a lasting one. A deep bench is one of the strongest defenses you have against worship team burnout.

The one-page sub sheet every sub needs

What a sub can do on Sunday is decided before Sunday, by what you handed them and when. Write one page, keep it the same for everyone, and update it once a season. Give it to each new sub the day you add them to the bench, not the night you need them.

Put all of it on the single sheet.

  • Songs, keys, and arrangement. The set list with the key for each one, plus a short note on the version you actually play, since your arrangement rarely matches the record.
  • Chart and track access. A link to charts or lead sheets, the exact recordings you play to, and the click or tracks if you run them.
  • Tech and monitoring. In-ears or wedges, how the click and tracks reach the player, and who runs sound for them.
  • Call time and the room. When to arrive, where to park, where to load in, and who meets them at the door.
  • Dress. How your team dresses on a Sunday, so no one stands out by surprise.
  • One contact. A single name and number for questions, and who leads from the platform.

A sub who gets this sheet early walks in as a teammate who did their homework. A sub left to guess spends the first song finding their footing in front of your church.

When your bench still comes up empty

Even a deep bench has a bad week. Three players out at once, a holiday weekend, a flu season that moves through your whole team. You did the proactive work and the seat is still open, and that is not a failure of leadership. It is an ordinary hard week in ministry.

This is the moment a staffing partner extends your bench overnight. Instead of filtering strangers at 9:00 on a Saturday, you get a vetted musician who plays your style, follows a leader well, cleared for skill and character before you ever met them, and ready on short notice. Your own bench stays the backbone. Outside help covers the week it cannot reach.

The church has always kept a bench

Depth on the worship team is not a modern staffing trick. When David organized the musicians for the temple, the roster ran to 288 who were trained and skilled, deep enough that their duties were assigned by lot, young and old, teacher and student alike (1 Chronicles 25:7-8). The ministry of music was built to outlast any one player having a hard week. Keeping a bench is simply how the people of God have guarded that ministry for three thousand years.

Common questions

- q: How do I build a worship sub bench?
Start with the people already around you. Your past fill-ins, the players one seat over, musicians in your own congregation, and names passed to you by worship leaders you trust. Gather two or three per hard seat, run each one through the same vetting you would give a regular, and rotate them onto ordinary Sundays so they stay current.
