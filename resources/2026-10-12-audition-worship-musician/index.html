What to Look For When You Audition a Worship Musician

A video can tell you someone plays. The audition tells you how they play with your team. Here is what to notice in the room, a 20-minute script you can run, and a printable scorecard for skill, teachability, reliability, spiritual fit, and serving the room.

Worship Connect Group ·October 12, 2026 ·7 min read

The video looked perfect. One minute of someone playing a worship song on their phone, clean tone, every note where it belonged, and you asked them to come in. Now they are standing in your quiet sanctuary on a weeknight, your drummer setting up behind them, and you have 20 minutes to learn the one thing the clip could never show. Not whether they can play. Whether they can play with the people already on your platform.

An audition is the only time you read a musician in real time before your church meets them on a Sunday. Used well, it shows you their skill, how they take direction, and whether they play for the room or for themselves. Here is what to look for, a simple script you can run in about 20 minutes, and a scorecard you can print and mark while they play.

What you are really testing for

The clip already answered the easy question. Anyone who sends you a clean video can play. The room is where you learn the rest of it. You find out if a player follows when you change an arrangement on the spot, if they leave room for the other musicians or crowd every bar, and how they handle a correction when it lands in front of other people. Your congregation never hears a scale. It hears whether the people on the platform are pulling together, and reading that in advance is the whole reason you booked the room.

Set the room up before they arrive

A fair session starts a few days early. Send the player 2 or 3 songs from a recent set with the keys and a link to the charts, and ask them to prepare one. Choose a second song and ask them to come in cold on it, so you can watch them read something new. Tell them how long it will run, who will be in the room, and that a drummer or an acoustic guitar will play alongside them. None of this is a hidden test. A musician who gets a clear heads-up shows you their real ceiling instead of their nerves. If you are still gathering names to invite in the first place, our guide to recruiting a worship team walks through finding them one person at a time.

A 20-minute audition script

You do not need a long session. You need the right 20 minutes. Run the same 5 steps with every player so you are comparing people and not moods.

  • The song they prepared. Let them play the one they chose, start to finish, with the click or an acoustic behind them. You learn their tone, their time, and how they carry a song all the way through without it unraveling.
  • The cold read. Hand them the chart for the second song and count it in. You are watching how fast they find the map and lock in, not how perfect every chord is.
  • A change on the fly. Partway through, pull the band down to a single instrument and the vocal for a verse, then build it back. Watch whether they lift their eyes and move with you or keep playing the version in their head.
  • One real correction. Ask for a specific adjustment. Softer in the verse, a simpler fill, a cleaner voicing. What they do in the next 30 seconds tells you more than anything they played before it.
  • A short conversation. 2 minutes off the instrument. Ask what worship means to them and why they want to be on your platform, and listen for humility more than polish.

A full vetting process reaches past the room into references, a background check, and a deeper faith conversation, and our complete guide to hiring worship musicians covers that whole path. This is the live piece you can only do face to face.

What to watch for while they play

The script gives you something to do. This is what to study while they do it.

Start with their eyes. A player who serves keeps looking up, at you and at the band around them. Eyes glued to the fretboard for a full song usually mean someone still wrestling the instrument rather than leading with it.

Then listen for the space. The best moment in the room is often the quietest one. A mature player leaves gaps, lets the vocal breathe, and never treats an empty bar as a problem to solve. Count what they leave out, not only what they put in.

Watch how they recover. Everyone misses. A chord clams. A come-in lands late. The question is what happens in the half second after, when a teachable player simply resets and stays with the room while a fragile one lets one wrong note drag the next three down behind it.

Last, check what they did with your correction. A musician who heard you, changed the part, and kept the change for the rest of the song is showing you the one trait that outlasts raw talent. A musician who nodded and played it their own way just told you how every rehearsal will go.

A worship audition scorecard you can print

Memory is generous to the last person who played and unfair to the first. Score each player on paper, in the moment, on the same five things, so you are weighing people against a standard instead of against your mood. Give each item a 1 to 5, where 1 means not yet and 5 means ready this Sunday, and jot a line of notes underneath. A player who lands mostly 4s and 5s is ready for a seat. Mostly 2s and 3s is often a development track rather than a flat no.

  • Skill. Can they hold time, play in your keys, and get cleanly through a song with a little pressure on them.
  • Teachability. Did they take your correction and actually apply it, without going cold or getting defensive.
  • Reliability. The quiet signals point here. Showing up on time, prepared, with working gear and the homework done.
  • Spiritual fit. Does their heart for worship and their walk with God match the role you are handing them in front of your people.
  • Serving the room. Did they play for the song and the congregation, leaving space and following your lead, instead of for themselves.

When the audition is over

Score it, thank them, and name where they landed plainly. When someone is not ready, be honest about why and show them the work that would close the gap. An honest turndown, handed over with care, lets a person come back and try again a year from now without any awkwardness. A yes gets a clear next step and a start date.

There is a reason this weighing is old. When Saul needed a musician, a servant recommended David by naming all of it at once. He knew how to play the lyre, he carried himself with courage and good judgment, and the Lord was with him (1 Samuel 16:18). Earlier in that same chapter, God reminds Samuel that people weigh the outward appearance while He weighs the heart (1 Samuel 16:7). A good read holds both together, the hands and the heart, and refuses to settle for only one.

Common questions

What should I have a musician play at an audition?
Give them 2 songs from a recent set. Ask them to prepare one so you hear them at their best, and have them read the second one cold so you see how fast they find a new chart. Then change one thing on the fly, like pulling the band down for a verse, to see if they follow your lead in real time.
How long should a worship audition be?
20 minutes usually does it. That gives you time for one prepared song, one cold read, a single correction to test how they take direction, and a short conversation about their heart for worship. A longer session rarely tells you more than a focused one, and it is easier to schedule several players in an afternoon.
Should auditions be in person or by video?
Use a video to decide who is worth meeting in person, then do the real read in the room. A clip proves someone can play, but it cannot show you whether they follow a leader, lock in with your drummer, or leave space for the song. Those only show up live, which is the whole point of meeting face to face.
